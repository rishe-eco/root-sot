Why coupled, not separate
In the NVC model you're drawing on, feelings and needs aren't two adjacent topics — they're two ends of one mechanism. A feeling is the signal; the need is what it's signaling about. Met need → pleasant feeling, unmet need → unpleasant feeling. So if you teach feelings vocabulary as a standalone module, you're not just splitting a curriculum, you're teaching a subtly wrong model: you'd be training people to read the gauge without knowing what the gauge measures. That's exactly the trap you intuited — someone getting fluent at labeling distress with no route to what it's pointing at. Your instinct that "matching from the beginning gives it context" is the correct one, and it's not just a UX nicety; it's fidelity to how the thing actually works.
On your worry that recognition-without-understanding could backfire
Worth knowing: the research on affect labeling (Lieberman, Eisenberger and colleagues) suggests naming a feeling actually down-regulates it — putting the emotion into words reduces amygdala activity. So bare recognition isn't inherently harmful; labeling itself is mildly regulating. The thing that does backfire is rumination — "why do I always feel this, what's wrong with me" — which is a different cognitive move from noticing-and-naming. That distinction is a design lever for you: build the module so the emotional move is notice → name → orient toward the need, which has forward motion, and steer hard away from notice → analyze → spiral, which doesn't. Coupling to needs is itself the anti-rumination mechanism, because it turns "I feel bad" into "I feel bad, and that's information about something I care about." So the coupling solves the very worry that made you consider merging them in the first place. Good news: your two impulses agree.
The sequencing insight (this is the real decision)
Your competence A (vocabulary) and competence B (distinguishing feelings-from-thoughts, needs-from-strategies) aren't peers — B depends on A. You can't teach someone that "I feel ignored" is a thought rather than a feeling until they have enough real feeling words to reach for instead (lonely, hurt, anxious). Same with needs vs. strategies — "I need you to text me back" is a strategy, and you can only see that as a strategy once you have the abstract need vocabulary (reassurance, connection, mattering) to name what's underneath it. So B is a refinement layer that sits on top of A, not a separate early lesson. Leading with "here's how you're naming your feelings wrong" is both premature and discouraging.
And here's the part that ties it together: the feeling-vs-thought distinction is actually the doorway to the need. The classic faux-feelings — ignored, abandoned, judged, manipulated, unsupported — are all thoughts about what someone else did, and every one of them is a signpost to an unmet need. "I feel ignored" → that's an interpretation → under it is maybe "hurt/lonely" (real feeling) → pointing at a need for connection or mattering. So B doesn't compete with the A-coupling; it reinforces it. That's a strong argument for keeping everything under one roof.
On the mindset framing — I think this is your best insight and I'd build the module around it
The frame you set before any vocabulary matters enormously, and there's evidence for it. Research on implicit theories of emotion (Tamir, and adjacent to Dweck's mindset work) finds that people who believe emotions are malleable and workable-with regulate better than people who treat emotions as fixed things that happen to them. And Lisa Feldman Barrett's constructed-emotion account says roughly the same from the mechanism side: emotions aren't verdicts delivered to you, they're built from raw affect plus the concepts you have available — which is why vocabulary expands regulation. That gives you both the frame ("emotions are signals you can get better at reading, not verdicts about you") and the empirical justification for the whole module: teaching granularity literally improves regulation (Barrett's emotional-granularity work links finer distinctions to less drinking, less aggression, better outcomes). So the module isn't "learn some words" — it's "build the conceptual resolution that makes emotions legible and workable." Worth putting that frame first, briefly, before any list of feelings.

Affect labeling (Lieberman's line of work): when someone attaches a word like "angry" to a feeling, amygdala activity decreases while the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex becomes more active. It produces effects like those seen during explicit emotion regulation, which is why it's described as a form of implicit emotion regulation — it doesn't even feel like effortful regulating while it's happening. So bare recognition isn't the danger you feared; the act of naming is mildly calming on its own. UCLA HealthSage Journals

Start in the body, before the words. There's a clean directional finding worth building on: improvements in interoception affect the ability to recognize one's own emotions, rather than the reverse. Body awareness is upstream of naming. And there's a concrete, teachable method here — Gendlin's "focusing," which the alexithymia literature leans on: you attend to a bodily sensation and test words against it; words that don't fit won't resonate, and once a word accurately matches the sensation, new insight and a felt shift tend to emerge. This matters for you in two ways. First, it gives you a non-analytic on-ramp — "where do you feel it, what's it like in your body?" before "what's it called?" — which sidesteps the rumination trap by keeping attention on sensation rather than on "why am I like this." Second, it makes the vocabulary feel like discovery rather than a quiz: the user finds the word that resonates instead of picking the right answer. FrontiersFrontiers
Teach the A-vs-B distinctions by contrast, not by definition. The way people learn a concept's boundary is by seeing minimal pairs that differ on exactly the critical dimension (variation theory / contrasting cases). NVC's "faux feelings" material is, structurally, already a contrast set built for this — you just present it as such. Faux-feeling words like "unheard," "unsafe," or "unloved" are thoughts about what others are doing to us; they carry unspoken blame, and it's more useful to identify the actual feeling underneath (scared, angry) and the need it points to. Same structure for needs vs. strategies: the classic example is mixing up a need like "support" with a strategy for meeting it — "I need you to help with the housework" versus "I need support." So B is taught as pairs: same situation, "I feel ignored" (thought/interpretation) next to "I feel hurt, and I think I need to matter to you" (feeling + need). The contrast is the teaching. And this is exactly why the module should be coupled — faux feelings are interpretations that point at unmet needs, so the feeling-vs-thought distinction is literally the doorway from feeling to need. You're picturing a stage-gate — grind the loop, unlock distinctions, that stage replaces the last, then storytelling replaces that. But the research points the other way. Granularity is built by repeated in-context labeling over time, not by completing a stage. So the feelings module isn't a course you finish; it's one short recurring practice that accretes layers and eventually graduates into Reflect. The bare loop never retires — distinctions and storytelling grow on top of it, inside the same practice. Once you see it as a spiral instead of a ladder, "how long do I repeat this" stops being the scary question, because repetition is the point, not the toll you pay to move on.
Let me paint it.
Day one — the frame, felt not told (~6 minutes, happens once)
You open Journey, tap Learn, choose Emotion & need language. No lecture. It asks you to think of a moment today when you felt "off" or "bad" — deliberately using the vague word. Then: where did you feel that in your body? You land on something — a tightness in the chest, say. Then it offers three or four words and asks you to try each one on: drained? disappointed? uneasy? You pick the one that fits closest. And then the only frame line it needs: notice that nothing about the moment changed — but naming it gave you something to hold. That's what this is. Emotions aren't fixed facts about you; they're your body's signal plus the word you reach for. A better word is a better handle. That's the malleability belief demonstrated instead of asserted — you just experienced the affect-labeling effect on yourself. That single experience does more than a paragraph about Barrett would.
A typical day — the bare loop (~3 minutes, daily-ish)
This is the whole practice for the first couple of weeks, and it stays the spine forever. One gentle nudge a day (plus on-demand whenever something's up):
Take a breath. What's here right now? → Where do you notice it — and what's the texture? Tight, warm, heavy, jittery, light? (sensation first, no emotion word yet — this is the interoception on-ramp, and it keeps you out of the "why am I like this" spiral because your attention is on the body, not the story) → Now reach for a word. Try one on — does it fit, or is it close-but-not-quite? (a small curated palette, weighted toward pleasant/met-need feelings early, expanding as you go) → If this feeling is pointing at something you care about, what might that be? (needs offered, never forced) → Anything small this makes you want to do or ask for? (optional — and that last line is the seam into Reflect).
Two to four minutes. That's it. And yes — you just repeat this. Not to unlock anything; because the repetition is the training. The honest answer to "how long" is: it becomes a standing micro-habit, the way stretching does. But there's a soft readiness threshold — call it roughly seven to ten loops across one to two weeks — after which you've accumulated a small personal vocabulary of feelings and needs you've actually used, not memorized. That base is what the next layer needs to exist.
Around week two — distinctions surface inside the loop (no new module)
This is the key move, and it answers your "once or multiple sessions, alone or attached" directly: distinctions are taught attached, contextually, on your own live material, many times over — never as a standalone drill. One day you're in the loop and you type "I feel ignored." The app catches it gently: "ignored" is usually a read on what someone else did — more a thought than a feeling. Underneath it, what's the feeling — hurt? lonely? anxious? And then: and what were you needing — to matter, to be included? You've just been taught feeling-vs-thought and been handed the bridge to the need, in six seconds, using your own words. Next time "abandoned" or "disrespected" shows up, it happens again. That's variation theory in the wild — you learn the boundary of the concept by seeing your own faux-feelings contrasted against real ones, spaced across real occasions. It has to be multiple distributed touches, because that's the only way a contrast becomes a reflex rather than a fact you can recite.
Around week three or four — storytelling, the long-form mode (~10–15 min, weekly-ish)
Now an occasional invitation appears: want to take a whole moment apart? This is the "gym day" to the daily stretch — infrequent, longer, deeper. You pick a real situation and walk its layers: what actually happened (observation, stripped of evaluation) → the first, loudest feeling → what's underneath it (the secondary emotion) → the needs in play, which are usually more than one and sometimes in conflict → what it clarifies. Ten to fifteen minutes, done maybe weekly or when something big lands.
